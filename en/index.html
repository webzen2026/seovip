    A quick overview of what SEOCHECKER actually checks and why it matters.

    What is SEO and why does it matter

    SEO (search engine optimization) is a set of steps that make a website easier for Google and other search engines to understand, so they can properly index it and show it to people searching for what you offer. It’s not a trick or a one-off fix — it’s ongoing care for a site’s technical foundation, content and structure.

    A site with good SEO usually loads quickly, works on mobile, has clearly named pages, and content that genuinely matches what people are searching for. A site without SEO basics can be beautiful and useful, yet search engines might not know it exists at all.

    Summary

    SEO doesn’t decide how a site looks — it decides whether anyone finds it at all.

    Title tag: the most important line a user will see

    The title tag is the page’s name, shown as the clickable heading in search results and as the browser tab’s title. It’s the first thing people notice in the results, and search engines treat it as a strong signal of what the page is about.

    The ideal length is roughly 30–65 characters — shorter wastes the available space, longer gets cut off in results. A good title contains the page’s main topic and is unique for every page on the site.

    Summary

    Every page should have its own, descriptive title — it’s the business card seen by both people and algorithms.

    Meta description: the text that decides the click

    The meta description is a short text shown under the title tag in search results. Google increasingly replaces it with its own snippet from the page, but if you write a good description that matches the content, the odds improve that it will be shown — and, more importantly, that people will read it and click.

    The ideal length is 70–160 characters. It works best when it clearly summarizes the page’s value, rather than reading like a list of keywords.

    Summary

    The meta description doesn’t directly affect ranking, but it strongly affects how many people click through.

    H1–H6 heading structure: why order matters

    Headings H1 through H6 create a hierarchy on the page, similar to an outline in a document. There should be only one H1, summarizing the page’s main topic, while further headings (H2, H3…) break the content into logical sections.

    Both search engines and screen readers rely on heading structure to understand what’s the main topic and what are subtopics. A page with no headings, or a chaotic structure, is harder to read for both.

    Summary

    Well-structured headings help search engines understand content faster and help people navigate the page more easily.

    Mobile view: why the viewport tag can’t be missing

    Most visits to a website today come from mobile, and Google has long evaluated primarily the mobile version of a page (so-called mobile-first indexing). The viewport meta tag tells the browser how to render the page on a small screen — without it, the display can shrink to the point of being unreadable.

    Besides the viewport, it’s worth checking font size, spacing between tappable buttons, and whether content overflows the screen.

    Summary

    If a page doesn’t work well on mobile, it will show up in search results regardless of how good the content is.

    HTTPS: security that’s also an SEO signal

    HTTPS encrypts the communication between browser and server, so no one along the way can intercept or alter the data. Besides security, it’s also one of the signals Google takes into account when evaluating a site, and browsers actively flag sites without HTTPS as “not secure”.

    Practically every host today offers HTTPS for free (for example via Let’s Encrypt), so there’s no reason to skip it.

    Summary

    HTTPS is standard today, not a bonus — its absence breeds distrust with both people and search engines.

    Image alt text: accessibility and SEO at once

    The alt attribute describes an image’s content in text — screen readers use it for blind users, it shows up when an image fails to load, and search engines read it too, since they can’t see the image content itself.

    A good alt text is short and descriptive, not stuffed with keywords. Purely decorative images can have an empty but present alt="" attribute, telling screen readers to skip the image.

    Summary

    Alt text isn’t just an SEO detail — it’s real help for people who can’t see the image.

    Open Graph: how your link will look on social media

    Open Graph tags (og:title, og:description, og:image) determine how a page’s preview looks when shared on Facebook, LinkedIn or elsewhere. Without them, the network has to guess the preview itself, which often ends up picking a random image or not showing a card at all.

    A filled-in, visually clear preview increases the odds that people notice it in their feed and click through.

    Summary

    Open Graph doesn’t affect search ranking, but it directly affects how well your content spreads on social media.

    Structured data: how to get rich results

    Structured data is code (most often in JSON-LD format) that tells search engines exactly what a page contains — a star-rated review, a recipe, questions and answers, business information, and so on.

    Thanks to it, an enhanced, visually richer result (a so-called rich snippet) can appear in search, drawing more attention than a plain blue link. It’s not a guarantee of a better ranking, but it does increase visibility.

    Summary

    Structured data helps search engines understand a page’s content precisely, not just by guessing from the text.

    robots.txt and sitemap.xml: how search engines find you

    The robots.txt file tells search engines which parts of a site they may crawl and which they may not. Sitemap.xml, on the other hand, works as a map of the site — a list of all the important URLs you want search engines to know about and index.

    Both files are usually placed at the domain root, and robots.txt can also link directly to the sitemap. Without them, search engines will still find the site, just more slowly and less thoroughly.

    Summary

    They’re not mandatory, but they make it significantly easier for search engines to navigate your site.

    Internal linking: why links within a site help

    Internal links are links leading from one page on your site to another page on the same site. They help people find related content, while also showing search engines which pages are important and how they relate to each other.

    A page with no links pointing to it from the rest of the site is harder to discover and harder to trust. A simple rule: whenever you mention a topic you’ve covered in detail elsewhere, link to it.

    Summary

    The more thoughtfully a site’s pages are linked together, the easier it is for both people and search engines to navigate.

    How long until SEO changes show results?

    SEO isn’t instant. Small technical fixes (title, meta description, alt texts) can show up in results within days to weeks, once the search engine recrawls and re-evaluates the page.

    Bigger changes — new content, building site trustworthiness, restructuring — often take weeks to months to show. The speed also depends on how often search engines visit the site.

    Summary

    SEO is a long game — the first results come gradually, not overnight.
